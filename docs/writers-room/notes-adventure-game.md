# Table-read notes: the adventure-game room

Notes to the Springfield, Nintendo and cop-show rooms. Quotes are verbatim. Where I suggest a splice, I say so.

## Scene 1: Opening to New York

**Best beats**
- **Open:** "Warm, plain, and full of hope" and a bare "Welcome to Pizza HQ." Every room kept them. Tommy's housekeeping cuts are unanimous, and I cut my "Plain cheese... That is your cover" line (see below).
- **Contessa's interruption** (Springfield, cop-show and my draft all place it right after "Slices are vanishing all over the world."): "Mamma mia! The whole world? And nobody tells Napoli?" Nintendo, you put her interruption after the deep-undercover speech. That makes it a reaction to something else, so it's weaker.
- **Springfield's "Pipe down, Contessa. I'm getting to the string."** is the best pivot. It tells the player the strings are coming.
- **Naming The Order.** Nintendo's logic is strongest: "A ticket prints. An **order**." Cop-show has the best button on it: "Printed right on the ticket. That's how good they are." It shows his theory is made of something ordinary, and he reads that as proof. I'd splice them.
  - Alt: "Someone is pulling the strings. [T] Every vanishing starts the same way. A ticket prints, one word on top: **ORDER**. / I call them The Order. They don't even hide it. That's how good they are."
- **Undercover premise.** Nintendo's silence-is-success logic is cleanest: "The best don't come back at all. That is **deep undercover**." Springfield's "Under a cover. Lid down, tape on." is the best pun.
  - Springfield, "if the tape goes on twice, that's deep undercover. Bottom of the stack" is too much machinery. A player won't follow "twice" or "bottom of the stack" in a small text box.
  - Splice: "You go **undercover**. Under a cover. Lid down." Then Nintendo's "Most come back with a report. The best don't come back at all."
- **Roster intros.**
  - Springfield and Nintendo: "Vinny Two-Fold. Off the streets. Nobody would take him indoors." It beats my "Folds in half. Never under pressure."
  - Springfield's "Lid's open. Get in the box." answers Vinny's "Wait, what?" and sends him out. It never says what happens next.
  - Nintendo's "Thickest stack of tickets on the board" gives New York a reason to go first.
  - Cop-show's "Only a senator needs all three" is a fine Phil intro.
  - Keep "get in close, and find out who" as Springfield and cop-show have it, in the last beat before the roster.

**Doesn't work**
- **My "Plain cheese, every one of you."** Contessa is margherita and Stan is deep dish, so the premise is false on sight. I concede and cut it.
- **Nintendo's** "Cardboard cover. Nobody looks twice at a pizza." makes the cover a joke that is already true.
- **Cop-show's** "Three words, on every one. Original Italian" is two words. Nintendo counted correctly.

## Scene 5: The Long Beach bridge

I'd go with Nintendo's logic: "Twelve disguises. Then one real name. You only drop the disguise at **home**." It is the best bridge in the round, for three reasons:
- It is plainly his reading.
- It is warm, which fits a gift for Katelyn.
- It makes the correctly spelled name the only clue he needs.

Parts from the other drafts:
- Springfield: "Twelve customers who can't spell it. One who can." It is the crispest setup.
- Stan as the skeptic, from Springfield and my draft: "Or she's just a person who can spell." Stan's flat voice carries it better than Phil (Nintendo) or Vinny (cop-show). The answer is "That's exactly what she'd want you to think." (Springfield, cop-show).
- Cop-show's "Nobody spells it right unless it's theirs" is a strong runner-up.

What I'm dropping:
- **My** "Does it end, or did you run out?" depended on a string scene the player won't remember. I concede.
- **Nintendo's** midnight note, "One of each. It's a party." It invents a note nobody established, so it fails the "premise holds" test unless Tommy adds the art.
- **Springfield's** "she wrote the others' names" is baroque, though funny.

My alt for the whole scene, trimmed to about ten lines:
```
wide:   OLD: Look at you. Look at what's left of you.
        OLD: Good. Nobody swings that hard at a bluff.
board:  OLD: Twelve tickets. Twelve spellings. Twelve disguises.
        OLD: Then Long Beach prints. One house. Spelled **right**. K-A-T-E-L-Y-N.
        DEEP: Or she's just a person who can spell.
        OLD: That's exactly what she'd want you to think.
        OLD: Twelve disguises, then one real name. You only drop the disguise at **home**.
wide:   JUMBO: A point of order. Will you be joining us in the field, sir?
        NAPLES: Sì. You, who see so much. You come, yes?
old:    DEEP: You never leave the building.  OLD: I left the building.  DEEP: When?
        OLD: ...1987. I've been in this box since before you were dough.
        OLD: I'm too stale for this shit.
```
The Springfield "1987" exchange beats my "I would love to, kid" and Nintendo's "oven before your grandparents were dough". A date is funnier than a gesture, and 1987 is the stale joke. The swear lands on a setup.
